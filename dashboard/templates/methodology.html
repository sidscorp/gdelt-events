Methodology

Exactly how everything on this dashboard is selected, ranked, and written — nothing hidden.

The data

Every 15 minutes the pipeline ingests the newest batch from the GDELT Project's Global Article List (~44,000 outlets worldwide). Articles are kept for a rolling 60 days. GDELT's crawl runs roughly 1–2 hours behind real-time publication, so brand-new stories first appear as single articles and accumulate their "covered by N sources" counts as syndication is crawled.

Event cards ("covered by N sources")

Every English article is embedded with a local neural model (nomic-embed-text, running on our own GPU). A clustering pass groups near-duplicate articles about the same event, so the feed shows one card per event instead of the same headline dozens of times. The card links every member article.

How the feed is ordered (Importance sort)

The default "Importance" ordering ranks each event in your selected time window by a composite score:

  • Coverage (weight {{ w_coverage or 0.5 }}) — log(1 + number of sources covering the event). More outlets = more important.
  • Recency (weight {{ w_recency or 0.3 }}) — exponential decay with a 12-hour half-life-style constant: a 12-hour-old event keeps ~37% of its recency weight.
  • Velocity (weight {{ w_velocity or 0.2 }}) — sources per hour since the event first appeared. Rewards stories breaking fast.

Each component is normalized within the current window's candidates, then combined. "Newest"/"Oldest" bypass this entirely and sort by crawl time. There is no editorial input and no personalization — every visitor sees the same ranking.

How topic pills decide what belongs (since July 2026)

Each curated topic pill works in two stages:

  1. Nomination (recall) — an article becomes a candidate for a pill if it matches the pill's keyword list or its embedding is semantically close to the pill's topic description.
  2. Judgment (precision) — every candidate is then read by an LLM judge ({{ judge_model or 'gpt-oss-120b' }}, an open-weights model, called via our self-hosted gateway) which answers one question: does this article actually belong to this topic? Only judge-approved articles get in. For the noisiest topics (Supply Chain, AI & Defense, OSS Vulnerabilities) borderline verdicts are rejected too.

This replaced pure keyword matching in July 2026 after a blind LLM audit measured how often each pill's articles were actually on-topic: Supply Chain scored just 25% under keywords alone. Every article's inclusion reason is stored and shown as a badge on its card in pill views (judge verdict + semantic similarity, or the matched keyword for non-English articles, which skip the judge).

Where it actually stands. Re-measured {{ eval_date or '26 August 2026' }} by sampling {{ eval_n or 40 }} articles per pill and having a model judge each one blind: precision ranges from {{ eval_min or '56%' }} to {{ eval_max or '95%' }}, median {{ eval_median or '76%' }} across {{ eval_pills or 16 }} pills. Supply Chain moved from 25% to {{ eval_supply or '72%' }}. That is a large improvement and it is not uniformly good: the strongest pills (Geopolitics & Conflict, FDA, Energy & Climate) sit at 91–95%, while Semiconductors ({{ eval_semi or '56%' }}), AI Governance ({{ eval_aireg or '60%' }}) and Cybersecurity ({{ eval_cyber or '61%' }}) are the weakest and are the next thing being worked on. We publish the current numbers rather than the flattering ones.

One honest caveat about timing: keyword tagging happens as articles are ingested, but judging runs on a cycle a few minutes behind it. So the very newest articles in a pill can briefly show a keyword badge before the judge has ruled on them, and a small number are later removed. What you see is judge-gated within minutes, not instantly.

Custom pills (for signed-in users) use pure semantic matching against your written description at the strictness you choose — no LLM judge, so expect a bit more noise than the curated pills.

How the AI briefing is written

When you open a briefing for a view + time window:

  1. The system takes the top {{ event_limit or 12 }} events by the same Importance score as the feed — the briefing summarizes exactly what the feed shows. Near-identical headlines are collapsed first, so those are 12 distinct stories.
  2. A prompt is built containing: the {{ event_limit or 12 }} numbered events (title + summary + outlet + source count, explicitly marked as importance-ranked), the titles the previous briefing covered (so the model writes progressions instead of re-explaining), any ongoing story threads it has been tracking, and a hard rule that the top-5 events must be covered.
  3. The prompt is sent to {{ model or 'gpt-oss-120b' }} (an open-weights model, via our self-hosted gateway). Citations like [3] link back to the exact numbered event.
  4. The result is cached per view/window, and how long it counts as fresh scales with the window (about {{ fresh_short_h or 3 }} hours for a 3-hour briefing, {{ fresh_long_h or 24 }} hours for a 30-day one) — the "cached" label and timestamp are shown under each briefing.
  5. The model's formatting varies between runs — one briefing cites [3], the next 【3】 — so the text is normalized before it is stored and again before it is drawn, and the citation markers are turned into the superscript links you see. The wording is never altered.

Click the ⓘ on any briefing to see everything that generation used: the model, the full ranked source list, the continuity context, and the verbatim prompt.

Why raw article counts mislead

The obvious way to rank news is to count articles. It does not work, for a reason worth understanding: GDELT indexes syndication, not events. A single wire story republished by 300 regional outlets produces 300 rows. A genuinely significant story covered thoughtfully by six major papers produces six. Sort by count and you rank syndication reach, which correlates with how cheap a story is to reprint rather than how much it matters.

That is why the feed ranks events rather than articles, and why the score combines three things instead of one. Coverage (log of distinct sources) captures breadth while damping the syndication effect — going from 5 outlets to 50 matters far more than 200 to 245. Velocity (sources per hour since first appearance) catches stories that are breaking right now, before their totals accumulate. Recency decays older events so a week-old story cannot sit on top of the feed forever on volume alone. Each is normalized within the current window's candidates, so the ranking answers "what is biggest in this time slice", not against all history.

The honest limitation: coverage is still a proxy for editorial attention, and editorial attention has its own biases — toward English-language sources, toward Western outlets, toward what is already trending. This system measures what the press is covering. That is not the same as what is important, and no amount of scoring fixes the difference.

What an "event" actually is

Every English article is embedded with a local neural model (nomic-embed-text, running on our own GPU — no article text is sent to a third party for this). A leader-clustering pass groups articles whose embeddings are near-duplicates: the first article becomes a cluster seed, and each subsequent article either joins an existing cluster it is close enough to or starts a new one.

This is deliberately a cheap algorithm rather than a sophisticated one. It is single-pass, order-dependent, and it will occasionally split one story across two cards or merge two similar stories that happened the same day. The alternative — full pairwise comparison — does not survive contact with 25 million rows. Every card links its member articles, so when the clustering is wrong you can see that it is wrong.

Why keyword matching was not enough

The topic feeds originally worked on keyword lists. In July 2026 a blind audit asked a language model, for a sample of each feed, whether each article actually belonged. The results were bad in a specific way: Supply Chain scored 25%. Keywords do not distinguish "Apple's chip supply chain" from "a recipe using apples", and the failure is worst exactly where the vocabulary is most ambiguous.

The fix separates two jobs that keywords were doing badly at once. Recall — finding candidates — still uses keywords plus semantic similarity to a written description of the topic. Precision — deciding membership — is now a language model reading the article and answering one question. Only approved articles enter the feed. Measured precision now spans {{ eval_min or '56%' }}–{{ eval_max or '95%' }} (median {{ eval_median or '76%' }}); see the pill section above for the per-pill picture, including which pills are still weak.

Worth recording, because it took six weeks to notice: that gate silently stopped working. From July until 22 August the judge only ever saw candidates nominated by semantic similarity — every keyword-matched article bypassed it entirely, because the check for "already decided" was accidentally the same query as the check for "keyword candidate". Supply Chain drifted back to 97% unjudged keyword matches. It was found by auditing this page's own claims against the database, and fixed on 22 August; the backfill re-judged 34,000 articles and Supply Chain shed about four fifths of its rows.

Two things worth being explicit about. Non-English articles skip the judge and are included on keyword match alone, so they are noisier. And a negative result worth recording: pure embedding similarity was tried as the membership test and failed — the separation between relevant and irrelevant articles was about 0.02 cosine, far too narrow for any threshold to be both precise and useful. Semantic similarity is good at nominating candidates and bad at judging them.

Where the briefings can still be wrong

Briefings are grounded: the model only ever sees the numbered events it was given, and it is instructed to cite by index. It cannot cite a source that was not in its list, which is why citation links cannot point somewhere invented. But grounding is not the same as correctness, and there are real failure modes:

  • The briefing inherits the ranking's biases. If the importance score surfaces the wrong {{ event_limit or 12 }} events, the summary faithfully describes the wrong {{ event_limit or 12 }} events.
  • It summarizes headlines and article summaries, not full article text. Nuance below that level is invisible to it.
  • Models paraphrase, and paraphrase can shift meaning — particularly around attribution, hedging and numbers. The citation next to each claim exists so you can check it.
  • Output formatting varies between runs of the same model; the text is normalized before display, but the wording is never edited.

The briefing is a reading aid for deciding what to open. It is not a substitute for the source.

What the AI does not do

  • No model chooses which articles enter the database or the global feed — that's coverage math only.
  • Briefings can only cite the numbered events they were given; they are instructed never to invent numbers or URLs.
  • No personalization, tracking-based ranking, or engagement optimization anywhere.
