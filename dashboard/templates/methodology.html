Methodology

How items on this site are selected, ranked, and written. All numbers below are drawn from the live system.

1. Data acquisition

Every 15 minutes the pipeline reads the latest batch from the GDELT Project's Global Article List (~44,000 outlets) and Global Knowledge Graph. Storage is a rolling 60-day window. GDELT's own crawl trails real time by roughly 1–2 hours, so a new story first appears as a single article and grows its coverage count as syndication is crawled after it. Ingestion, tagging, ranking, and the global feed are deterministic code; no model decides what enters the database.

2. Event clustering

Each English article is embedded with nomic-embed-text on our own GPU; no article text leaves the site for this. A single-pass leader-clustering algorithm then groups near-duplicate articles into one event: the first article seeds a cluster, and each later article joins a cluster or starts one.

This algorithm is chosen cheap on purpose. It is order-dependent, and it occasionally splits one story into two cards or merges two same-day stories. Full pairwise comparison does not run at 25 million rows; linking every member article from its card is the compromise, so wrong merges are visible rather than hidden.

3. Feed ranking (Importance)

The default sort ranks events in the selected window by a composite score with three components, each normalized within that window's candidates:

  • Coverage — log(1 + sources). Weight {{ w_coverage or 0.5 }}.
  • Recency — exponential decay; an event 12 hours old keeps ~37% of its recency weight. Weight {{ w_recency or 0.3 }}.
  • Velocity — sources per hour since first appearance. Weight {{ w_velocity or 0.2 }}.

"Newest" and "Oldest" bypass the score and order by crawl time. Every visitor sees the same ranking: there is no personalization and no engagement-based ordering.

Why not count articles: GDELT indexes syndication. A wire story reprinted by 300 regional outlets produces 300 rows; a story covered by six major papers produces six. Article counts track how cheap a story is to reprint. Logging the source count damps this (5 → 50 outlets matters; 200 → 245 does not), and velocity catches stories still breaking. Remaining bias: coverage measures editorial attention, which skews toward English-language and trending material. The score measures what the press covers; deciding what matters is left to the reader.

4. Topic pills

Each curated pill selects in two stages:

  1. Nomination: an article is a candidate if it matches the pill's keyword list or its embedding is close to the pill's written topic description.
  2. Approval: an LLM judge ({{ judge_model or 'gpt-oss-120b' }}, an open-weights model hosted by Fireworks and called via our self-hosted gateway) reads each candidate and answers one question: does this article belong to this topic? Only approved articles are tagged. Borderline verdicts count as rejections for the noisiest pills (Supply Chain, AI & Defense, OSS Vulnerabilities).

The two-stage form exists because a July 2026 blind audit found Supply Chain at 25% precision under keywords alone: "Apple's chip supply chain" and "a recipe using apples" look the same to a keyword list, and vocabulary like that is exactly where the failures clustered. Pure embedding similarity was also tried as the approval test and abandoned: relevant and irrelevant articles separated by about 0.02 cosine, too narrow for any threshold to hold both precision and recall. Embeddings nominate; the judge approves.

Measured precision as of {{ eval_date or '26 August 2026' }} ({{ eval_n or 40 }} sampled articles per pill, judged blind): {{ eval_min or '56%' }} to {{ eval_max or '95%' }}, median {{ eval_median or '76%' }}, across {{ eval_pills or 16 }} pills. Supply Chain moved from 25% to {{ eval_supply or '72%' }}. Strongest pills: Geopolitics & Conflict, FDA, Energy & Climate at 91–95%. Weakest, and being worked on next: Semiconductors ({{ eval_semi or '56%' }}), AI Governance ({{ eval_aireg or '60%' }}), Cybersecurity ({{ eval_cyber or '61%' }}).

Three operational notes. First, tagging runs at ingest but judging runs minutes behind, so the newest articles may briefly carry a keyword badge before a verdict arrives; a fraction are later removed. Second, non-English articles skip the judge and go in on keyword alone, so pill feeds are noisier outside English. Third, from July to 22 August the judge bypassed all keyword-nominated articles because the "already decided" check was the same query as the "keyword candidate" check; the regression was found by auditing this page's claims against the database, the backfill re-judged 34,000 articles, and Supply Chain shed about four fifths of its rows. That maintenance offset is why the numbers above are re-measured rather than assumed.

Custom pills (signed-in users) use semantic matching against a written description at a chosen strictness. There is no judge in that path, so expect more noise than the curated set.

5. Briefings

For a view and window, the pipeline:

  1. takes the window's top {{ candidate_limit or 40 }} distinct events by the Importance score (near-identical headlines collapsed first);
  2. has an editor model read all {{ candidate_limit or 40 }} and select about {{ event_limit or 10 }}, writing one line per choice and rejecting items that are not news (section indexes, job ads, listicles), with an explicit instruction to keep at least one consequential under-covered story;
  3. builds the writer's prompt from the selected events, the editor's reasons, the titles the previous briefing covered, and any ongoing threads;
  4. sends it to {{ model or 'gpt-oss-120b' }} (Fireworks-hosted, via our self-hosted gateway) and caches the result per view/window — roughly {{ fresh_short_h or 3 }}h fresh at a 3-hour window, {{ fresh_long_h or 24 }}h at 30-day, with the timestamp shown under each briefing.

Citations such as [3] are stitched to the numbered events by index; the model has no list beyond those, so a citation cannot point to an event it was not given. The ⓘ control on any briefing shows the model used, all {{ candidate_limit or 40 }} candidates, the editor's chosen and rejected lists with reasons, the continuity context, and the verbatim prompt. If the editor call fails, the briefing falls back to the top {{ event_limit or 10 }} by Importance and the panel marks the fallback.

Run-to-run formatting varies between the same model, so text is normalized before storage and again before display (citation markers become the superscript links). Content is never rewritten in this step.

6. Where briefings can still be wrong

  • Input bias transfers: if Importance nominates the wrong {{ candidate_limit or 40 }} events, the briefing describes a selected subset of a wrong window.
  • The editor judges from a headline and a one-line summary, not the full article; its own choices are published in the ⓘ panel for disagreement.
  • Summaries are of headlines and descriptions only. Anything living below that level is invisible to the model.
  • Paraphrase shifts meaning at the edges (attribution, hedging, numbers); the [n] next to a claim is the check.
  • Format varies between runs; text is normalized, wording isn't.

Use the briefing as a triage layer for deciding what to open. The citation is there to be clicked.
